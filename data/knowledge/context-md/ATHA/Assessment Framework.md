There is not yet one approved detailed assessment framework. The documents contain three overlapping versions:
- The five viability lenses.
- A seven-criterion judging model.
- An eight-criterion hackathon-style rubric.
The cleanest solution is to make the five viability lenses the formal assessment framework and place the useful elements of the seven- and eight-criterion versions underneath them.
Proposed detailed assessment framework
1. Assessment objective
Judges are assessing the quality of the team’s viability judgement—not whether the team produced the most exciting idea, advanced prototype or polished presentation.
A high-performing team should demonstrate that it:
- understood the enterprise problem;
- gathered and evaluated relevant evidence;
- tested rather than defended its assumptions;
- used AI appropriately and transparently;
- identified uncertainty and missing information;
- considered implementation and organisational realities;
- reached a proportionate recommendation;
- can defend that recommendation under questioning.
A “do not proceed” recommendation can receive the highest score if it is better supported than another team’s positive recommendation.
2. Scoring structure
Assessment area|Weight|
Desirability|15%|
Feasibility|20%|
Viability|20%|
Scalability|15%|
Responsibility|20%|
Decision integrity and transparency|10%|
Total|100%|
Feasibility, viability and responsibility receive slightly greater weight because they are the areas most likely to distinguish a plausible enterprise opportunity from an attractive but unsuitable concept.
3. Desirability — 15 points
Core question: Does this solve a meaningful problem for identifiable users and stakeholders?
Judges should assess:
Problem understanding
- Is the underlying problem clearly defined?
- Has the team distinguished the problem from the proposed solution?
- Is the problem material enough to justify intervention?
- Has the team identified who currently experiences the problem?
Stakeholder and user evidence
- Who are the users, buyers, beneficiaries and affected stakeholders?
- What evidence shows that they need or value the proposed outcome?
- Has the team considered conflicting stakeholder interests?
- Has it avoided relying entirely on imagined personas or unsupported claims?
Current alternatives
- How is the problem managed today?
- Why are current processes, products or non-AI alternatives insufficient?
- Could a simpler non-AI intervention solve the problem more effectively?
Adoption
- What would encourage or prevent adoption?
- Does the proposal fit existing workflows?
- What behavioural, cultural or trust barriers exist?
- Is there a credible reason that users would change their behaviour?
A strong submission demonstrates real demand and explains why AI is relevant. A weak submission assumes that novelty automatically creates demand.
4. Feasibility — 20 points
Core question: Can this AI opportunity realistically be delivered with the available data, technology, capabilities and constraints?
Judges should assess:
Appropriateness of AI
- Is AI necessary or materially better than a rules-based or conventional solution?
- Is the selected AI capability appropriate for the task?
- Has the team avoided inserting AI simply because the event concerns AI?
Data feasibility
- What data is required?
- Does it exist, and can it legally and practically be accessed?
- Is its quality, volume, representativeness and timeliness sufficient?
- What data gaps, labelling requirements or ownership restrictions exist?
Technical feasibility
- Is the proposed technical approach credible?
- Can it integrate with the organisation’s current systems?
- What infrastructure, security and interoperability requirements exist?
- Are model accuracy, reliability and failure modes understood?
- What assumptions still require technical testing?
Delivery capability
- Does the organisation have the necessary people and skills?
- What external vendors or specialists would be required?
- Is the timeline realistic?
- What dependencies could prevent delivery?
A strong submission identifies specific technical constraints and proposes a realistic validation path. A weak submission describes possible AI capabilities without showing how they could operate in the partner’s environment.
5. Viability — 20 points
Core question: Does pursuing this opportunity make organisational and economic sense?
Judges should assess:
Value creation
- What measurable value could the opportunity create?
- Could it increase revenue, reduce costs, manage risk, improve quality or save time?
- Who receives the value?
- Is the claimed value material to the organisation?
Cost and resource requirements
- What would development, integration, operation and governance cost?
- Are ongoing costs included, not just initial implementation?
- What people, training, procurement and process changes would be required?
- Has the team acknowledged hidden or indirect costs?
Business case
- Are benefits and costs supported by reasonable evidence?
- What assumptions drive the business case?
- How sensitive is the conclusion to changes in those assumptions?
- Is there a plausible route to return on investment or strategic value?
Organisational fit
- Does the proposal align with the partner’s strategy and operating model?
- Is there a credible internal owner?
- Which functions would need to cooperate?
- Could procurement, governance, budget cycles or organisational politics prevent adoption?
A strong submission provides a conditional business case and states what evidence must be obtained before investment. A weak submission presents benefits without implementation costs or organisational constraints.
6. Scalability — 15 points
Core question: Can the opportunity move beyond a successful experiment into sustained organisational use?
Judges should assess:
Pilot-to-scale pathway
- What should the first pilot test?
- What would count as success or failure?
- What evidence would justify expansion?
- Are the pilot boundaries and decision gates clear?
Technical scalability
- Can the infrastructure support greater volume and complexity?
- Will data pipelines, model performance and monitoring remain reliable?
- What integration or architecture limitations may appear at scale?
Operational scalability
- Can the operating process be repeated across users, teams or locations?
- What training and support would be required?
- Does scaling create additional staffing or governance burdens?
Transferability
- Is the opportunity useful only in one narrow context?
- Could it extend to other products, departments, regions or organisations?
- Which elements are genuinely reusable, and which require local redesign?
A strong submission includes a phased roadmap with explicit evidence gates. A weak submission treats a successful pilot as proof that organisation-wide deployment will work.
7. Responsibility — 20 points
Core question: Can the opportunity be pursued safely, fairly and accountably?
Judges should assess:
Privacy and security
- What personal, confidential or commercially sensitive data is involved?
- How will access, storage, retention and sharing be controlled?
- What cybersecurity risks could the AI system introduce?
Fairness and harm
- Who could be disadvantaged by errors or bias?
- Is the data representative of affected populations?
- Could the system reinforce existing inequalities?
- How severe are false positives, false negatives or other failures?
Explainability and contestability
- Can affected people understand important outputs?
- Can decisions be challenged or corrected?
- Does the required level of explainability match the seriousness of the decision?
Human oversight
- Which decisions should remain with humans?
- Who is accountable for the AI-supported outcome?
- Are escalation, override and incident processes defined?
- Could automation bias lead people to trust the system excessively?
Legal and governance readiness
- What regulation, policy, intellectual-property or contractual constraints apply?
- What approvals would be needed?
- How would the system be monitored after deployment?
- Is there a credible process for stopping or changing it?
A serious unresolved responsibility issue should prevent a recommendation for full deployment, regardless of the total numerical score.
8. Decision integrity and transparency — 10 points
Evidence quality — 4 points
- Are important claims supported by credible evidence?
- Has the team separated evidence, inference and assumption?
- Are sources traceable?
- Has contradictory evidence been acknowledged?
- Has the team identified what it could not verify?
Transparent AI use — 3 points
- Is an AI audit log provided?
- Does it record the tools used, important prompts, outputs and human verification?
- Has the team checked AI-generated claims and sources?
- Is it clear where AI influenced the analysis?
- Are limitations, hallucinations and rejected outputs documented?
Defence and communication — 3 points
- Is the recommendation clear and proportionate?
- Can the team explain its reasoning without hiding behind slides?
- Does it answer questions directly?
- Can it identify what evidence would change its conclusion?
- Does every major claim connect to the analysis?
Presentation polish should carry little weight. It should never compensate for weak evidence or reasoning.
9. Scoring scale
Each criterion should use the same five-level scale:
Score|Meaning|
1|Unsupported or materially flawed|
2|Some relevant thinking, but major evidence or reasoning gaps|
3|Plausible and reasonably supported, with important conditions|
4|Strong, evidence-based and implementation-aware|
5|Decision-ready, highly rigorous and resilient under challenge|
Judges should record both a score and a short justification. Numbers without written reasoning will not provide meaningful feedback or support moderation.
10. Final recommendation categories
Teams should select one of four outcomes:
Proceed
Evidence supports moving directly into a defined implementation or advanced pilot phase.
Conditional pilot
The opportunity appears promising, but specified assumptions must be tested before greater investment.
Reframe or investigate further
The problem may be valuable, but the proposed approach, data, scope or operating model requires substantial revision.
Do not proceed
The opportunity is not sufficiently desirable, feasible, viable, scalable or responsible under current conditions.
Every recommendation must state:
- the level of confidence;
- the decisive evidence;
- the most important unresolved assumptions;
- the principal risks;
- the conditions that would change the conclusion;
- the next decision gate.
11. Assessment safeguards
The final judging process should include several safeguards:
- Judges receive the framework and calibration examples before the event.
- All judges use the same scoring form.
- Mentors do not score teams they have substantially supported, where possible.
- Enterprise partners may clarify facts and assess organisational relevance, but should not control the full score.
- Judges score independently before deliberation.
- Material scoring differences are discussed during moderation.
- Each team receives a lens-by-lens assessment, not merely an award result.
- A serious responsibility failure cannot be offset by presentation or commercial potential.
- A missing or materially incomplete AI audit log makes a team ineligible for the overall award.
- Innovation and teamwork may support special recognition, but should not distort the viability decision.
This framework reconciles the existing documents by retaining problem understanding, AI integration, technical feasibility, business value, user experience, implementation, responsible AI and presentation quality—but placing each under SIGNAL’s five-lens methodology rather than operating three competing rubrics.
